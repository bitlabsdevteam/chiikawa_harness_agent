02 / Good coffee begins inland

The coast is home.
The grower comes first.

Goa gives this concept its pace and palette. It does not automatically give the coffee its origin. Much of India's coffee is grown in the southern hills, and each cup carries decisions made long before roasting: shade, harvest timing, sorting, fermentation and drying.

Our sourcing standard is simple: say what is known, distinguish it from what is hoped for, and make room for the people doing the agricultural work. A romantic region name is not traceability.

  • Names, not borrowed stories.A producer or supplier should be named with permission. No invented family farms, direct-trade relationships or feel-good impact numbers.
  • Specific lots, not permanent promises.Harvests change. A future release should state the process, available crop details and a cup description based on that actual coffee.
  • Clear prices, before commitment.Packaging, delivery, tax treatment and subscription terms must be visible before any real payment. This planner takes none.

04 / The kitchen notebook

Less guesswork.
More good cups.

A scale helps, but attention matters more. Start with clean equipment and pleasant-tasting water. Change just one variable between brews so you can taste what changed.

A bright, unhurried pour.

A paper filter gives a clean cup and lets delicate aromas through. Medium-fine grounds should look a little finer than granulated sugar. Start here with Chikmagalur light or Araku afternoon.

Coffee / water16 g / 256 g
Water92–96°C
Total time2:30–3:30
  1. Rinse the paper with hot water and discard the rinse. Add coffee, then gently shake the brewer to level the bed.
  2. Start a timer. Pour 45 g of water evenly over the grounds and wait 30–40 seconds. This bloom helps trapped gas escape.
  3. Pour slowly in two or three stages until the scale reads 256 g. Keep the stream gentle and avoid pouring directly down the paper walls.
  4. Let the water drain, remove the brewer and swirl the cup. Taste once it cools a little; very hot coffee can hide both sweetness and faults.

Next cup: if it tastes thin and sour, try a slightly finer grind. If it feels dry and bitter, go a little coarser. A slow drawdown can also mean a clogged filter, not simply a dark roast.

A full-bodied, easy morning.

The French press keeps more oils in the cup. Use a medium-coarse grind rather than huge, uneven chunks. Coorg or Nilgiri is a comfortable place to begin.

Coffee / water30 g / 480 g
Water92–96°C
Total time6–8 minutes
  1. Warm the glass with hot water, empty it and add your grounds. Set the press on a stable, heat-safe surface.
  2. Add all 480 g of water, saturating the grounds. Rest the lid on top without pushing the plunger down. Wait four minutes.
  3. Stir the floating crust gently. Scoop off excess foam if you prefer a cleaner cup, then allow two to four minutes for grounds to settle.
  4. Lower the plunger slowly without forcing it. Pour gently into cups or a separate server rather than leaving brewed coffee on the grounds.

Next cup: muddy texture often comes from fine particles and rough pouring. Letting the coffee settle can help more than grinding dramatically coarser.

A little pot, a deeper cup.

A moka pot makes concentrated coffee, not espresso. Pot sizes vary, so use the manufacturer's fill level rather than forcing a fixed recipe. A medium-fine grind works well with Coorg or After the monsoon.

CoffeeLevel basket
WaterBelow valve
HeatLow–medium
  1. Check the gasket and safety valve. Fill the base with water to the maker's marked level, never covering the valve. Warm water is optional; use a cloth if the base is hot.
  2. Fill the basket loosely and level the grounds. Do not tamp them or obstruct the filter. Assemble securely according to the pot's instructions.
  3. Place over low-to-medium heat, keeping the handle away from the flame. Stay nearby and watch for a steady flow of coffee into the upper chamber.
  4. Remove from heat when the flow turns pale or begins to sputter. Let pressure subside before opening. Dilute the brewed coffee with hot water or warm milk to taste.

Next cup: a harsh, burnt taste can mean the pot stayed on the heat too long. Keep the valve clear and never open a pressurised pot.

For the warmest afternoons.

This is a ready-to-drink starting recipe, not a strong concentrate. Use a clean covered container and brew in the fridge, especially in Goa's heat. Medium-coarse coffee keeps filtering manageable.

Coffee / water60 g / 720 g
TemperatureRefrigerated
Steep12–16 hours
  1. Add grounds and cool, drinking-quality water to a clean container. Stir until every patch of coffee is wet.
  2. Cover, refrigerate and note the start time. Begin tasting at twelve hours rather than assuming a longer steep is always better.
  3. Strain through a fine sieve, then a rinsed paper filter if you prefer less sediment. Transfer to a clean, covered bottle and keep refrigerated.
  4. Pour over ice and dilute only if it tastes too strong. Make small batches and use within two to three days; discard coffee with an unusual smell or appearance.

Next cup: if it feels flat, try a shorter steep or a brighter coffee. Cold extraction changes flavour; it does not make the drink caffeine-free or shelf-stable.

05 / Notes from the coast

Not a beach souvenir.
A morning companion.

MARÉ takes its name from the Portuguese word for tide. The visual language borrows the rhythm of tiled verandas, shuttered windows and the changing light after rain. Goa is a living place, not just a holiday backdrop; the idea is to make something at home on an ordinary kitchen shelf.

“Let the day gather slowly.”

That is our own little reminder, not a testimonial. You might drink your coffee before a commute, between work calls or beside yesterday's newspaper. There is no correct coastal lifestyle to buy into. The ritual only needs to fit your day.

The proposed range stays Indian-grown rather than borrowing the glamour of distant origins. When a coffee eventually earns a place on this shelf, its agricultural story should be more precise than the illustration on its bag.

06 / Keep the good in the bag

Freshness is a habit.
Not a countdown.

Heat, moisture and repeated exposure to air are the enemies. On a humid coast, a few small storage choices matter more than a beautiful jar.

FIELD NOTE / 01

Close it. Shade it.

Keep coffee sealed in a cool, dry cupboard, away from the hob and direct sun. Squeeze excess air from a resealable bag before closing it. A clear jar on a sunny windowsill looks lovely but gives your beans the wrong kind of coastal holiday.

Do not store an everyday bag in the fridge: repeatedly opening cold coffee in humid air can encourage condensation. Keep your scoop dry, too.

FIELD NOTE / 02

Let the roast settle.

Freshly roasted coffee releases gas. Some rest can improve an even extraction, particularly for espresso. A few days to two weeks is a useful exploration window, not a universal instruction; roast style, packaging and brew method all change the answer.

A future bag should carry its roast date and a practical starting recommendation. Follow your cup rather than treating the youngest bag as automatically the best one.

FIELD NOTE / 03

Buy for your rhythm.

Divide your weekly coffee dose by the bag weight before subscribing. Two 16 g cups a day use 224 g a week, so a single 250 g bag will not last a couple all month. Ground coffee loses aroma faster; smaller sealed portions can help.

If you freeze spare whole beans, seal them in small airtight portions. Let a portion come fully to room temperature before opening, and avoid repeatedly thawing the same bag.

A little clarity goes a long way.

Choose the right grind.

The planner offers whole bean, pour-over, French press and moka grinds. Match the selection to the equipment you own. If you use a South Indian metal filter, espresso machine or another brewer, do not assume these settings will fit; a real roaster should confirm that grind with you.

08 / A few things worth asking

Coffee questions.
Straight answers.

No secret vocabulary required. These answers cover the proposed range and the planner. For a first brew, the kitchen notebook above is a more useful companion than an expensive new gadget.

Can I buy these coffees right now?

Not here. MARÉ is an original roaster concept with indicative profiles and pricing. You can make and download a plan, but there is no stock reservation, checkout, payment or subscription enrolment. A live release would need confirmed lots, a real seller and complete terms first.

Is the coffee grown in Goa?

The identity is Goan; the proposed coffees are not labelled Goa-grown. The cards distinguish growing regions from processing geography. A future monsooned coffee would need both its growing source and the location of monsooning disclosed, rather than treating the coast as a farm.

Are the tasting notes added ingredients?

No. Notes such as cocoa, pear and almond describe possible sensory impressions from unflavoured coffee. They are not ingredient or allergen statements. Here they are intended profile directions, not verified cupping results. Any actual packaged product would still need its own ingredient and handling information.

How does the annual saving work?

The annual proposal costs 90% of twelve monthly payments. For The Drift, ₹750 × 12 is ₹9,000; a 10% reduction makes ₹8,100 upfront, equivalent to ₹675 per month. The ₹900 saving is for the whole year. The switch updates all three tiers and any subscription already in your plan.

Which coffee should I try with milk?

Start with the proposed Coorg or monsooned profiles for cocoa, nuts and more body. Brew a little stronger before adding milk rather than choosing an extremely dark roast by default. The lighter profiles are worth tasting black first so their subtler differences are easier to notice.

Does a subscription plan select an origin?

Yes. Once a tier is added, an origin selector appears in your bag planner. The proposed rate is the same across these six profile options, and all monthly portions use that one selection. This records a preference, not a guarantee that a future crop will be available.
